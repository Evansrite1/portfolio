How to use the keyboard

  1. Pick a sound. Piano, electric piano, organ, synth or strings. All five are made inside your browser, so there is nothing to download.
  2. Play. Click or tap the keys, slide across them for a glissando, or type on your computer keyboard. A S D F G H J K L are the white keys and W E T Y U O P are the black keys.
  3. Change range. Use the octave buttons or the Z and X keys. Hold Space or press Sustain to let notes ring.

How to find the key of a song

  1. Play the song and press Listen with microphone. The tool hears it through your microphone, so keep the speaker near and the room quiet.
  2. Or listen to a tab. On a computer in Chrome or Edge, choose a browser tab that is playing the song and tick Share tab audio. This is cleaner than a microphone.
  3. Or drop in a file. The whole song is analysed on your device in a few seconds.
  4. Read the result. You get the key, how sure the tool is, the next most likely keys, and the notes it heard. The scale lights up on the keyboard, and you can play it to check by ear.

How to break a song down and learn from it

  1. Drop a song into Song breakdown. It is analysed on your device. A 4 minute song takes about 10 to 30 seconds.
  2. Read the timeline. Sections show the shape of the song, such as verse and chorus. Chords show the harmony with its Roman numerals. The lead line is the most prominent melody, usually the voice. Bass shows the low notes, and beat hits show kick, snare and hi-hat style hits.
  3. Press play. A green line moves across the timeline. The box under the keyboard shows the section, chord, bass note and lead note right now, and the keyboard lights up with them. Click the timeline to jump to any part.
  4. Read the notes under the timeline. They explain the chord, what the bass is doing and how the lead note relates to the chord, so you learn why it sounds the way it does.

Roman numerals are a way to name chords by their place in the key. In C major, C is I, F is IV and G is V. Lower case, like vi, means a minor chord. Many hit songs use a few of the same numerals in different orders.

What it can and cannot do. This works by listening to the sound and finding the notes, not by separating the song into individual instruments. That means the bass and chords are usually right, the tempo and sections are good guides, and the lead line is the most prominent melody, which can be a guitar or a keyboard when they are louder than the voice. It cannot split a choir into soprano, alto and tenor. Use it as a map, and check against your ears.

How the key finder works

The tool turns the sound into a spectrum, picks out the strong peaks and groups them into the 12 notes of the scale, C to B, whatever octave they were played in. That gives a profile of how much each note was used. It then compares that profile with the typical profiles of all 24 major and minor keys and shows the closest match. This is the same idea used by DJ software and music apps.

A major key and its relative minor use exactly the same notes, for example C major and A minor. When the two are close, the tool shows both so you can pick by ear: the key that the song feels like it rests on is the right one.

Tips for a better answer

  • Use a full-band section with chords, not only drums or a spoken intro.
  • Let it listen for at least 15 seconds. Songs that change key halfway give a mixed answer.
  • Music with a lot of distorted noise or very low bass can fool any key finder. Try a different part of the song.

Frequently asked questions

Is it free?

Yes. There is no sign-up and no limit. It runs in your browser.

What sounds does the keyboard have?

Piano, electric piano, organ, synth and strings. They are generated by your browser, so they load instantly and work offline once the page is open.

How do I find the key of a song?

Press Listen with microphone while the song plays, or share a browser tab that is playing it, or drop in an audio file. After 10 to 20 seconds the tool shows the most likely key.

How accurate is the key finder?

It is right most of the time for songs with clear chords and melody. It can be wrong for songs that change key, have heavy noise or very little harmony. A relative major and minor, such as C major and A minor, share the same notes and are the most common mix-up, so the tool shows both when they are close.

Can it listen to Spotify or YouTube?

Yes. Open the song in another tab, press Listen to a browser tab, choose that tab and tick Share tab audio. This works in Chrome and Edge on a computer. On a phone, use the microphone.

Is my audio uploaded?

No. The microphone, the tab audio and the files are all analysed on your own device.

Why does the microphone need permission?

Your browser asks before any page can listen. The tool only listens while you have it running, and the stop button turns the microphone off.

Does it work on a phone?

The keyboard works with touch, and the microphone key finder works on most phones. Listening to a tab is a computer feature.

What is the song breakdown?

It listens to a song you upload and draws a timeline: the sections, the chords, the lead line, the bass notes and the beat. When you press play, the keyboard lights up with the chord, bass note and lead note you are hearing, and the tool explains them in plain words.

Can it separate the singer from the instruments?

Not exactly. It finds the most prominent melody and shows it as the lead line, which is usually the voice. It does not split a song into separate audio tracks, and it cannot separate soprano, alto and tenor parts of a choir.

How accurate is the song breakdown?

The tempo, key, bass notes and chords are usually right for songs with clear instruments. Sections and the lead line are good guides but can be wrong, especially in busy mixes or songs that change tempo. It is a learning map, so check it against your ears.

Are my songs uploaded?

No. The song is read and analysed on your own device, and nothing is sent to a server.

What does the confidence mean?

It shows how much better the top answer matches than the next different key. A low value means the music fits more than one key, so listen longer or try another part of the song.
